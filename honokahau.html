Honokahau

Honokahau

by Perry Hotchkiss

Shelley awoke to the twittering of bird songs nearby in the coffee and mango trees. She would always be thankful for this way to meet the day. Hawaii Island was so unique. It was big, separated from the other Hawaiian Islands by distance and a wild stretch of ocean, and thousands of miles from any continent. In the 1970's, it was just beginning to be known by travelers and those coming to check it out from the mainland. One could still "disappear" into the jungle or forest, and live simply in peace and tranquility. If one had a way to make some income, the rest seemed provided by giving and sharing, and taking a hand at some gardening. Coffee shacks in Captain Cook, Kealakekua, and Honaunau, were very basic, but still could be leased with acreage for less than $150.00 a month. (Some of the ones here for a while actually got on a list and were given a 99 year bishop estate lease for little or no money. What a blessing that must have been!) Many of the old coffee farms were established over the last hundred years by Filipino, Chinese, Portuguese, or Hawaiians. Some of these farms were inherited, but many came up again with the passing of the old farmer and family.

Besides the "Belt Highway" that circled the island, ancillary secondary roads connected the different small and larger communities. On the "Kona Side" at that time, there were jungle trails from one person's place to another, and through various holdings to the highway or beach. Of course the beach at Kealakekua Bay was a focal point and meeting place for many in the community. It had a somewhat rocky beach, but could be used, with care. The main way to the beach from the highway, was down idyllic and beautiful Napoopoo Road, which wound down through giant Jacaranda trees covered with purple blossoms. Walking down the road covered with these purple blooms, was an other-worldly experience never forgotten by anyone. Small side roads branched off Napoopoo on the miles down to the bay. These roads led to small communities nestled on the vast slope of the tropical environs. At this time, with clear skies and beautiful weather, those gathered on their lanais for the sunset, all could experience a phenomena rarely seen anywhere else. When the sun reached the ocean, for a minute or less, one could see "the green flash", as the sun dipped below the horizon! It was a singularly beautiful and enjoyable experience for old and young, locals and newcomers alike. Seeing it was somehow a "bonding experience" to many, appreciated but not necessary to be understood.

At that time, the little villages of Honaunau, Captain Cook, Kealakekua, and Kainaliu were where folks shopped in the varied little shops and stores, art galleries and eateries, and even a couple of theaters; the one in Kainaliu, the only one remaining open then. From a person's house or lanai, at night you could hear the wild pigs dining on fallen fruit; with avocados the most available as the whole Kona slope had hundreds of old avocado trees. Resourceful people could work as a couple and harvest the large avocados by the dozens. Useful was a potato sack or other strong container, a nimble climber with a clipper if required, and an alert partner to catch the avos thrown down from above. This was an important function, where the "catcher" had to be 100% in the moment, and not only aware of the location of the "harvester", but when the fruit was on the way. (The avocados were often firm and weighed at least a pound. Daydreaming, and being hit in the head by one of the tasty projectiles, could knock a person out cold. (I've seen it happen more than once!) The catcher then carefully bagged the avocados, and got ready for the call of the next arrival of a gift from on high. Sometimes a friend and I would get 30 to 50 pounds in an afternoon. We would keep those most ripe, and sell the others to the health food store or small markets. The land provided if one had some ingenuity.

The "City of Refuge" on the coast at Honaunau, was a sacred site to Hawaiians, a place to respectfully visit, and share the beauty of the tide pools, breaking waves, and the historic area. (Now it is a national park.) From the path along the shore, giant shoals of a beautiful fish called a yellow tang, could be seen and enjoyed. When checking out the tide pools, it was good to watch out for moray eels and the beautiful, but deadly, blue octopus. Shore birds were present, and sometimes quick mongoose and crabs. The coastline was used for fishing, picking of opihi, (a delicacy), and gathering materials for weaving or art work. Some fishing by small boat was done in areas with calmer waters usually being the case. A visitor soon found out the difference between swimming in California for example, with it's miles of sandy shelf before the deep, and Hawaii, where a six foot wave could be getting a push from an ocean with thousands of feet of deep water just off shore. The difference is startling and could often be deadly. One afternoon, Shelley and her boyfriend Harris, went on an adventure to the area north of Kailua-Kona town, near the small boat harbor at Honokahau. The beach was reached at the northeast of the harbor, and was a nice stretch of sand in front of coconut and kiawe trees, a good place to sunbathe and swim in the shallows. They arrived at the parking lot, unloaded their kayak from the top of the Jeep Commander, had a juice, gathered their gear for spear-fishing, and went to the north end of the small beach lugging their craft. It and their gear were not a problem for a couple of healthy kamaaina, and they slipped away from the shore. Wave action was small, and the area was somewhat protected. A few folks said hi to them from their lounging positions, as they trundled past with their boat. It was a hot but beautiful day in Hawaii nei.

They paddled off shore a ways north, and out from the beach. Shelley could still see the pandannas hats worn by a couple at the end of the beach sitting in partial shade reading. All was serene and calm and looked like a great time to gather some tasty fish. She stayed in the kayak, and stayed on alert and ready to receive the fish that Harris speared, take them into the boat and put them on the stringer line. Her job was not especially easy, but she was practiced at her part of their operation. He patted her foot, slipped over the side, was first handed his fins, then his mask, then his spear gun. He set it in the water, waved and disappeared below. He came up twice for breath and went down again. His first catch was a menpachi, a sort of rare red fish, and they both were pleased as these brought a good price at the small markets. Another time up for air and then down. After a bit, he surfaced with a mid-sized ahi, and was jazzed, as the tuna was good eating. Harris rested with one hand on the kayak's bow while she dealt with his last catch. He submerged again, and came up pretty quickly with a 3 foot barracuda thrashing about. She handed him a rubber mallet, and he pounded it a few times on it's head against the kayak's hull. He gave it to Shelley, and she carefully put it on the stringer, and over the side with the others. He mentioned, "that one was hassling me probably because of blood in the water from our prior catches. If they start to gather or sharks show up, we should call it a day." She said, "I agree Sweetie. Stay safe." He went below, and the ocean shook! Harris quickly surfaced, and looked up as a twenty foot high monster wave was about to break on him! He had a moment to look for Shelley, and he saw the kayak at the top of the wave, and her diving off the back side! His only choice was to quickly get in low and under the breaking wave! He barely made it, as it broke, folded down, and crashed toward the beach. When he surfaced after a long time, he was being pulled out to sea in an enormously strong rip current. He swam away as he could, at 90 degrees from it and parallel to the beach. As he continued to swim, he slowly pulled away from the massive rip, he looked for Shelly and the kayak! Then he spotted her about 500 yards farther out hanging onto their closed plastic cooler. Using more strength than he thought he had, he swam hard for Shelley. He knew that he was making progress and was closer to her as he yelled "hang on, Honey." She waved in acknowledgment, but looked very tired. He had released his spear gun, had his mask ripped off his head, but still had on his two swim fins and his dive knife attached to his right calf. Within a few minutes, he joined her and said, "try to float on your back to conserve some energy." She nodded, did so, but held onto the handle of the cooler. He realized that they were still being pulled out to sea.

Harris said, "Shelley, we have to swim parallel to the beach, but angling in toward shore. Release the cooler now and swim as hard as you can for as long as you can! I'll be right next to you so try not to worry so." She gasped, "Okay honey", grabbed a juice from the cooler, drank some and handed it to him. He was amazed that she did that, took it, drank the rest for energy, and they went at it. Later, they paused, and he figured that they weren't being sucked out any longer, but were about a mile off shore. Good, but far from great. He said, "come here and hang onto me, I have the fins." She laughed and said, "do you think anyone saw us and is coming for us?" He said, "I hope so Honey, but not so far. You're a trooper and doing real well. I'm proud as can be of my Sweetheart." "Tell me more about that if there is a later;" she said with a sigh. Grudgingly, they started toward shore, and both later said they were so tired that it seemed like swimming through molasses. Then they heard a boat horn and looked around, and from the small boat harbor, a small fishing boat was headed their way! They were reached quickly, and a man jumped in to help them to the ladder, and up on board! He and she collapsed on the deck, while thanking their rescuers. Jack, the skipper said, "We saw you in the wave as it hit, and thought that you were toast. It's a true miracle that you two are alive! Must be good living!" Harris said, "I think you're right," and reached for Jack's hand who pulled him to his feet. A crewman helped Shelley to a sitting position on a giant plastic cooler. They were given big towels since most of their clothes were gone. As they reached their slip at the harbor, it was incredible that more damage was not done by the rogue wave. Three small boats had been sunk, and a few of different size were straddling the wharf.

Later they learned that there were 5 injuries in the harbor, but no deaths. Unfortunately, three beach goers were washed up into the trees, then pulled out to sea. Their bodies were never found. The wave seemed to have hit mostly that section of coast, with little damage anywhere else. Luckily Harris had a hide-a-key for the Jeep inside a bumper. They had been given free outfits at the harbor tourist shop and food and drinks at the grill courtesy of Jack the skipper and his crew. They all were so happy that Shelley and Harris made it. They looked on the beach, but couldn't find more of their kayak than sandwich size pieces. Oh well. As they cruised back toward Kailua, then home, he said,"Honey lets pick avos for a while and forgo our fishing adventures." She said, "I'm for dominos on the lanai with you and a cold beer." He replied, "Sure, and luckily we have a chance to see the "Green Flash" once more Sweet One."

OM Namaste

— The End —
